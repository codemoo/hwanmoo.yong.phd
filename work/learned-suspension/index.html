Semi-active suspension control with deep reinforcement learning

Research·2019–20·with an automaker

A fixed suspension tune is always a compromise: what absorbs a sharp bump is wrong for a rough road. A semi-active suspension can adjust its dampers while the car drives, but the hand-written rules the industry relies on struggle when the road changes. An automaker asked whether a controller could learn the job itself through deep reinforcement learning, using only the low-cost accelerometers a production car can carry.

I split the problem by road type, because ordinary roads and sharp impacts call for very different reward functions. I trained a separate Soft Actor-Critic controller for each, and a detector that watches the body's motion for impacts switches smoothly between them. Training also modeled a real damper's limits: it can only absorb energy, and it responds with a lag.

The ordinary-road controller was trained and tested on a real car, not on a simplified model like the demo above. I built the in-car training software, which updated the controller on the spot from data logged while driving and blended in a rule-based controller to keep early learning safe. In a comparison drive, the learned controller cut the body's front-to-back rocking (pitch) by nearly a quarter compared with fixed dampers, more than the rule-based controller achieved on its own.

The work was published in IEEE Transactions on Industrial Electronics, with me as first author. I am also co-inventor on a granted patent, "Suspension control system based on reinforcement learning."
